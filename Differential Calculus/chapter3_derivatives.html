3.1

The Derivative and the Tangent Line Problem

One question drives all of calculus: how steep is a curve at a single point?

The big idea

Zoom in until the curve looks straight

A straight line has one slope everywhere. A curve doesn't, so we ask for its slope at one point. Zoom in on a smooth curve and it starts to look like a straight line. The slope of that line is the derivative. That line is the tangent line.

The tangent line problem

From secant line to tangent line

We can only compute a slope from two points. So take the point \(P=(a,f(a))\) and a second point \(Q=(a+h,\,f(a+h))\) a distance \(h\) away. The line through them is a secant line:

Slope of the secant line (rise ÷ run)
\[ m_{\text{sec}}=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]

Now slide \(Q\) towards \(P\) by letting \(h\to 0\). The secant line swings round and lands on the tangent line. Its slope is the limit:

Slope of the tangent line
\[ m_{\text{tan}}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]
\(h\)Secant slope

The numbers settle on one value as \(h\) shrinks. That value is \(f'(a)\).

\(y=f(x)\) secant line through \(P\) and \(Q\) tangent line at \(P\)
Definitions to memorise

The derivative

Derivative at a point
\[ f'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]
if this limit exists. Then \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\).
Alternative form (put \(x=a+h\))
\[ f'(a)=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} \]
Same idea: the second point is \(x\), and \(x\to a\).
Derivative as a function
\[ f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]
A new function that gives the slope of \(f\) at every \(x\).
Tangent and normal lines at \(x=a\)
\[ \text{Tangent: } y-f(a)=f'(a)\,(x-a) \] \[ \text{Normal: } y-f(a)=-\frac{1}{f'(a)}\,(x-a) \]
The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent, so slopes multiply to \(-1\).

Notation: all of these mean the same thing

\[ f'(x) \;=\; y' \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}f(x) \;=\; D_x\,y \]

Read \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) as "the tiny change in \(y\) divided by the tiny change in \(x\)": a limit of rise over run.

Worked examples

Using the definition, step by step

Example 1Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=x^2+2x\), then the tangent and normal lines at \(x=1\).
\[ f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2+2(x+h)-(x^2+2x)}{h} \] \[ =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cancel{x^2}+2xh+h^2+\cancel{2x}+2h-\cancel{x^2}-\cancel{2x}}{h} \] \[ =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h\,(2x+h+2)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h+2) \]
\(f'(x)=2x+2\)

At \(x=1\): \(f(1)=3\) and \(f'(1)=4\).

Tangent: \(y-3=4(x-1)\Rightarrow y=4x-1\)
Normal: \(y-3=-\tfrac14(x-1)\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac14x+\tfrac{13}{4}\)

Equal scales on both axes, so you can see the tangent and normal meet at a right angle.

The pattern is always the same: expand, cancel, factor out \(h\), cancel \(h\), then put \(h=0\).

Example 2\(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\)

Multiply by the conjugate to remove the roots from the top:

\[ \frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}} \] \[ \xrightarrow{\;h\to0\;}\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \]
\(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x},\ x>0\)

At \(x=0\) the slope blows up: the tangent there is vertical, so \(\sqrt x\) is not differentiable at 0.

Example 3\(f(x)=\dfrac1x\)

Combine the fractions on top first:

\[ \frac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac1x}{h}=\frac{x-(x+h)}{h\,x(x+h)}=\frac{-1}{x(x+h)} \] \[ \xrightarrow{\;h\to0\;}\; -\frac{1}{x^2} \]
\(f'(x)=-\dfrac{1}{x^2}\)

Always negative: the graph of \(1/x\) goes down everywhere.

Example 4Alternative form: \(f(x)=x^3\), \(a=2\)
\[ f'(2)=\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^3-8}{x-2}=\lim_{x\to2}\frac{(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)}{x-2} \] \[ =\lim_{x\to2}(x^2+2x+4)=12 \]

Use \(a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\) to factor.

Exam favourite: spot the derivative. Some limits are derivatives in disguise. Match them to \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) and read off \(f\) and \(a\).

What the derivative means

Slope = instantaneous rate of change

Average rate of change on \([a,b]\)

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \]

The slope of the secant line. "On average, how fast did it change?"

Instantaneous rate of change at \(a\)

\[ f'(a)=\lim_{b\to a}\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \]

The slope of the tangent line. "How fast is it changing right now?"

MotionA car's position is \(s(t)=t^2+3t\) metres after \(t\) seconds.

Average velocity on \([0,2]\): \(\dfrac{s(2)-s(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac{10-0}{2}=5\) m/s.

Velocity is the derivative of position: \(v(t)=s'(t)=2t+3\), so at \(t=2\) the speedometer reads \(v(2)=7\) m/s.

Graph of f versus graph of f′

Watch the slope draw a new graph

Drag the point along \(f\). Its tangent slope becomes the height of the green graph underneath. That green graph is \(f'\).

\(f\) going up ↗

tangent slopes positive, so \(f'(x)>0\): green graph above the axis.

\(f\) going down ↘

tangent slopes negative, so \(f'(x)<0\): green graph below the axis.

Peak or valley —

tangent is horizontal, so \(f'(x)=0\): green graph crosses the axis.

Differentiability

Where the derivative does not exist

\(f'(a)\) fails to exist when there is no single, non-vertical tangent line at \(a\). Four pictures to recognise:

Corner: \(|x|\) at 0. Slope \(-1\) on the left, \(+1\) on the right.

Cusp: \(x^{2/3}\) at 0. Slopes shoot to \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\).

Vertical tangent: \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) at 0. Slope is infinite.

Discontinuity: a jump. Not even continuous.

Theorem

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

Equivalently: not continuous ⇒ not differentiable. But the converse is false: \(|x|\) is continuous at 0 yet not differentiable there.

One-sided derivatives

\[ f'_-(a)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]
\[ f'_+(a)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]

\(f'(a)\) exists exactly when both one-sided derivatives exist and are equal. This is how you test a piecewise function at the point where the pieces meet.

Exam typeFind \(a\) and \(b\) so that \(f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2, & x\le 2\\ ax+b, & x>2\end{cases}\) is differentiable at \(x=2\).

1. Continuous (the pieces must meet): \(\;2^2=2a+b\;\Rightarrow\;2a+b=4\).

2. Same slope from both sides: left slope \(=(x^2)'=2x\) at \(2\) is \(4\); right slope \(=a\). So \(a=4\).

Then \(b=4-2(4)=-4\).

\(a=4,\ b=-4\)

Both conditions are needed. With \(a=2,\ b=0\) the pieces still meet (continuous) but the slopes are \(4\) and \(2\): a corner.

\(a=4,b=-4\): smooth ✓

\(a=2,b=0\): corner ✗

3.2

Differentiation Rules

Shortcuts that give the same answer as the limit definition, in one line.

The rules

Seven rules that do almost everything

Rule 1 · Constant
\[ \frac{d}{dx}[c]=0 \]
A flat line has slope zero.
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\pi^2]=0,\ \ \frac{d}{dx}[7]=0\)
Rule 2 · Power
\[ \frac{d}{dx}[x^n]=n\,x^{n-1} \]
Bring the power down, then lower it by one. Works for any real \(n\).
\(\frac{d}{dx}[x^7]=7x^6,\ \ \frac{d}{dx}[x^{-2}]=-2x^{-3}\)
Rule 3 · Constant multiple
\[ \frac{d}{dx}[c\,f]=c\,f' \]
Constants just come along for the ride.
\(\frac{d}{dx}[5x^3]=5\cdot3x^2=15x^2\)
Rule 4 · Sum & difference
\[ (f\pm g)'=f'\pm g' \]
Differentiate term by term.
\((x^4-x+9)'=4x^3-1\)
Rule 5 · Product
\[ (f\,g)'=f'\,g+f\,g' \]
"Derivative of the first × second, plus first × derivative of the second."
\((x^2\sin x)'=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x\)
Rule 6 · Quotient
\[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'=\frac{f'\,g-f\,g'}{g^2} \]
"Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared." Order matters!
\(g(x)\ne0\)
Rule 7 · Reciprocal (from Rule 6)
\[ \left(\frac{1}{g}\right)'=-\frac{g'}{g^2} \]
The quotient rule with \(f=1\), so \(f'=0\).
\(\left(\frac{1}{x^2+1}\right)'=-\frac{2x}{(x^2+1)^2}\)
Power rule, visually

See \(x^n\) and its derivative \(n\,x^{n-1}\) side by side

\(y=x^n\) \(y=\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1}\)

Check a few: where the blue curve is flat the green one is 0; where blue rises steeply green is large.

Rewrite first, then use the power rule

Roots and fractions are powers in disguise: \(\sqrt[m]{x^k}=x^{k/m}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{x^k}=x^{-k}\).

Why the product rule has two terms

The product rule is an area picture

Think of \(f\cdot g\) as the area of a rectangle with sides \(f\) and \(g\). Nudge \(x\) a little: \(f\) grows by \(\Delta f\), \(g\) grows by \(\Delta g\). The extra area is

\[ \Delta(fg)=\underbrace{g\,\Delta f}_{\text{green}}+\underbrace{f\,\Delta g}_{\text{amber}}+\underbrace{\Delta f\,\Delta g}_{\text{tiny red}} \]

Divide by \(\Delta x\) and let \(\Delta x\to0\). The tiny corner vanishes (it is "small × small"), leaving

\[ (fg)'=f'g+fg' \]

So it is not \(f'g'\): you would be keeping only the tiny corner and throwing away both big strips.

Rules in action

Polynomial\(y=3x^4-5x^2+7x-2\)
\[ y'=12x^3-10x+7 \]

Rules 1–4, term by term.

Roots & fractions\(y=\sqrt x+\dfrac{1}{x^2}-\sqrt[3]{x^2}\)
\[ y=x^{1/2}+x^{-2}-x^{2/3} \] \[ y'=\tfrac12x^{-1/2}-2x^{-3}-\tfrac23x^{-1/3} \]
Product\(y=(x^2+1)(x^3-2x)\)
\[ y'=\underbrace{2x}_{f'}\,\underbrace{(x^3-2x)}_{g}+\underbrace{(x^2+1)}_{f}\,\underbrace{(3x^2-2)}_{g'} \] \[ =5x^4-3x^2-2 \]

Check: expanding first gives \(x^5-x^3-2x\), whose derivative is the same.

Quotient\(y=\dfrac{x^2-1}{x^2+1}\)
\[ y'=\frac{(2x)(x^2+1)-(x^2-1)(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2} \] \[ =\frac{2x\,[(x^2+1)-(x^2-1)]}{(x^2+1)^2}=\frac{4x}{(x^2+1)^2} \]

\[ (fg)'(2)=f'(2)g(2)+f(2)g'(2)=(-1)(4)+(3)(5)=11 \] \[ \left(\frac fg\right)'(2)=\frac{f'(2)g(2)-f(2)g'(2)}{[g(2)]^2}=\frac{(-1)(4)-(3)(5)}{16}=-\frac{19}{16} \]
Horizontal tangentsWhere does \(y=x^3-3x^2-9x+5\) have a horizontal tangent?

Horizontal means slope \(0\), so solve \(y'=0\):

\[ y'=3x^2-6x-9=3(x-3)(x+1)=0 \] \[ x=-1\ \text{ or }\ x=3 \]

Points: \(y(-1)=10\) and \(y(3)=-22\).

\((-1,\,10)\) and \((3,\,-22)\)

The red dashed tangents are flat exactly at the top of the hill and the bottom of the valley.

Common mistakes.
  • \((fg)'\ne f'g'\). Test with \(f=g=x\): \((x^2)'=2x\), but \(f'g'=1\).
  • \(\left(\frac fg\right)'\ne\frac{f'}{g'}\), and the quotient-rule numerator is \(f'g-fg'\) in that order; swapping flips the sign.
  • The power rule needs a constant exponent: it works for \(x^5\), not for \(5^x\) or \(x^x\).
  • Simplify first when you can: \(\dfrac{x^3+x}{x}=x^2+1\) (for \(x\ne0\)) has derivative \(2x\), no quotient rule needed.

3.3

Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Everything comes from one picture (the unit circle) and one limit.

The key limits

Why \(\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\to1\)

\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x}=0 \]
Only true when \(x\) is in radians. All of calculus with trig assumes radians.

On the unit circle, compare three lengths for the same angle \(x\):

sin x: the vertical drop from the point to the axis
x: the arc length (radians measure arc length!)
tan x: the vertical line touching the circle

The picture shows \(\sin x\le x\le\tan x\). Divide by \(\sin x\) and flip:

\[ \cos x\le\frac{\sin x}{x}\le1 \]

As \(x\to0\), \(\cos x\to1\), so \(\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\) is squeezed to \(1\).

Derivation

Proof that \((\sin x)'=\cos x\)

Use the definition and the addition formula \(\sin(x+h)=\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h-\sin x}{h} \] \[ =\lim_{h\to0}\Big[\sin x\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\cos h-1}{h}}_{\to\,0}+\cos x\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\sin h}{h}}_{\to\,1}\Big]=\cos x \]

The same steps with \(\cos(x+h)=\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h\) give \((\cos x)'=-\sin x\). The other four follow from the quotient rule.

\(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\)

\[ (\tan x)'=\frac{\cos x\cdot\cos x-\sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x}=\frac{1}{\cos^2x}=\sec^2x \]

uses \(\cos^2x+\sin^2x=1\)

\(\sec x=\dfrac{1}{\cos x}\)

\[ (\sec x)'=-\frac{(-\sin x)}{\cos^2x}=\frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\sec x\tan x \]

reciprocal rule

The six formulas

Learn three, get six

FunctionDerivativeCo-functionDerivative
\(\sin x\)\(\cos x\)→\(\cos x\)\(-\sin x\)
\(\tan x\)\(\sec^2 x\)→\(\cot x\)\(-\csc^2 x\)
\(\sec x\)\(\sec x\tan x\)→\(\csc x\)\(-\csc x\cot x\)
Memory trick. Learn the left column. For the right column, put "co" on every function (sin↔cos, tan↔cot, sec↔csc) and add a minus sign. Every "c" function (cos, cot, csc) has a negative derivative.

See it: the slope of \(\sin x\) traces out \(\cos x\)

Bonus (preview of 3.6): keep differentiating \(\sin x\) and you go round this circle, back to \(\sin x\) every 4 steps.

E.g. the 27th derivative: \(27=4\cdot6+3\), so it is 3 steps round: \(-\cos x\).

Worked examples

Trig derivatives with the 3.2 rules

Product\(y=x^2\sin x\)
\[ y'=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x \]
Product\(y=\sec x\tan x\)
\[ y'=(\sec x\tan x)\tan x+\sec x\,(\sec^2x) \] \[ =\sec x\tan^2x+\sec^3x \]
Quotient\(y=\dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\)
\[ y'=\frac{\cos x(1+\cos x)-\sin x(-\sin x)}{(1+\cos x)^2} \] \[ =\frac{\cos x+\overbrace{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}^{=1}}{(1+\cos x)^2}=\frac{1}{1+\cos x} \]
Trig limitsUse \(\frac{\sin\square}{\square}\to1\)
\[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin5x}{3x}=\frac53\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin5x}{5x}=\frac53 \] \[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan x}{x}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos x}=1 \]
Tangent lineFind the tangent line to \(y=\cos x\) at \(x=\frac{\pi}{3}\).

Point: \(y=\cos\frac\pi3=\frac12\).

Slope: \(y'=-\sin x\), so \(m=-\sin\frac\pi3=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

\(y-\frac12=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\left(x-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\)
Horizontal tangentsWhere on \([0,2\pi]\) does \(y=x+2\sin x\) have a horizontal tangent?
\[ y'=1+2\cos x=0\;\Rightarrow\;\cos x=-\tfrac12 \] \[ x=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\ \text{ or }\ x=\tfrac{4\pi}{3} \]

cos is negative in quadrants II and III; reference angle \(\frac\pi3\).
